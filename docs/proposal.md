Large language models are increasingly used to search and summarize news, yet when generating without grounding they hallucinate — asserting claims unsupported by any source — precisely in a setting where factual accuracy matters most. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) mitigates this by conditioning the model on retrieved evidence, but most deployed RAG systems retrieve text alone, even though news is inherently multimodal: photographs document events, people, and places in ways the accompanying text often understates or omits. This limitation motivates a multimodal approach that grounds summarization in both textual and visual evidence and measures whether doing so improves factual accuracy. The central research question is whether multimodal retrieval improves factual summarization compared to text-only retrieval. We hypothesize that multimodal retrieval improves faithfulness most for event-centric stories, where images carry identity, location, and scene information, and least for abstract topics such as policy or markets.


This project frames the question as a controlled comparison: given a natural-language query or a source article, retrieve supporting evidence from a news corpus and generate a summary, varying only the retrieval configuration — text-only versus fused text-and-image retrieval. We use two public data sources. The first is the GoodNews dataset, approximately 466,000 New York Times image–article pairs with captions (2010–2018), from which we index a stratified subset of roughly 50,000 articles, keeping embedding, indexing, and inference within a single free-tier GPU and a modest LLM API budget. The second is a subset of the Visual News dataset, which provides image–article pairs from The Guardian, BBC, USA Today, and The Washington Post, used to test robustness under domain shift across outlets. Article passages are embedded with a sentence-transformer text encoder and images with CLIP (ViT-B/32); both are stored in a FAISS vector index. At query time, the system retrieves the top-k passages — and, in the multimodal condition, images with their captions — and passes them with the query to an instruction-tuned language model (e.g., Llama-3-8B-Instruct or GPT-4o-mini) that generates a summary constrained to the retrieved evidence and abstains when retrieval confidence is low. The full pipeline is deployed as an interactive demo (Streamlit on Hugging Face Spaces).


We compare two baselines — LLM summarization without retrieval and text-only RAG — against multimodal RAG. Systems are evaluated on claim-level faithfulness and hallucination rate (RAGAS-style entailment of each summary claim against retrieved evidence), retrieval recall@k, and ROUGE-L, using held-out article splits with near-duplicate filtering to prevent evidence leakage between the retrieval index and the test set; automatic factuality scores are validated against a small human-annotated subset. Ablation studies will quantify the effect of the number of retrieved items, the image–text fusion weighting, and caption availability on summary faithfulness; results will additionally be stratified by story type to test our hypothesis directly, with qualitative case studies of failure modes such as misleading retrieved images. This project integrates multiple course topics, including multimodal learning, large language models, retrieval-augmented generation, and conditioning through retrieved evidence. The project aims to determine whether visual grounding measurably reduces hallucination in news summarization, and to deliver — independent of the comparison's outcome — a deployed multimodal retrieval-augmented summarization system together with a reproducible factuality-evaluation harness. The work divides among three members: one owns retrieval and indexing, one owns generation, guardrails, and deployment, and one owns the evaluation harness, human annotation, and analysis.
